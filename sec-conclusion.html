16 A Workflow Checklist

The preceding chapters each took one part of a meta-analysis apart. This closing chapter puts the parts back together as a sequence of decisions, flags the mistakes that most often undermine published meta-analyses, and ends with a compact analysis template that runs the core steps on one dataset. It is meant to be used as a checklist while planning, analyzing, and reviewing.

16.1 Before the analysis

A meta-analysis inherits every weakness of the review that feeds it. The decisions below should be fixed in a protocol and registered before the data are seen (Chapter 2).

  1. Question. State the population, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcomes, and study designs. Decide whether the target is a single common effect or the mean and spread of a distribution of effects, because that choice drives the model (Chapter 4).
  2. Protocol and registration. Register on PROSPERO or OSF. Specify the primary outcome, the effect-size metric, the model and heterogeneity estimator, planned moderators, the handling of dependent effects, and the sensitivity analyses. Deviations are allowed; undisclosed deviations are not.
  3. Search and screening. Search several databases plus registries and grey literature, screen in duplicate, and record counts for the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.
  4. Extraction. Pilot the form, extract in duplicate, and keep the raw statistics (means, SDs, counts, test statistics) rather than only computed effect sizes, so that every effect size can be recomputed (Chapter 3).
  5. Risk of bias. Rate each study with a design-appropriate tool (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2) and plan how the ratings will enter the synthesis.

16.2 Choosing the model

Table 16.1 maps common data situations to the approach this book recommends and the chapter that develops it.

Table 16.1: Data situations, recommended approaches, and where they are covered.
Situation Approach Chapter
One effect per study, studies differ in design or population Random-effects model, REML or Paule-Mandel tau^2, Hartung-Knapp CI, prediction interval 4
Very few studies (k < 5) Bayesian model with an informative or weakly informative prior on tau; report sensitivity 9
Binary outcome with rare events or zero cells Mantel-Haenszel, Peto, or binomial GLMM instead of continuity corrections 4
Several effects per study, sampling covariances unknown CHE working model with robust variance estimation (CR2, Satterthwaite df) 6
Effects nested in studies nested in labs or papers Three-level model; RVE on top if the dependence structure is uncertain 6
Moderators of interest Mixed-effects meta-regression with Knapp-Hartung tests; permutation tests; few pre-specified moderators 5
Suspected selective publication Funnel plot plus a triangulation of PET-PEESE, selection models, and sensitivity analysis 7
Influential or outlying studies Influence diagnostics, leave-one-out, GOSH; report results with and without 8
Three or more competing interventions Network meta-analysis, checking transitivity and consistency 10
Correlation matrices and a theoretical path model Two-stage or one-stage MASEM 11
Correlated outcomes with known within-study correlations Multivariate random-effects model 12
Participant-level data available One-stage or two-stage IPD meta-analysis separating within- and across-study interactions 12
Sensitivity and specificity of a test Bivariate random-effects (Reitsma) or HSROC model 13
Estimates from quasi-experiments with different estimands Pool only comparable estimands; design as moderator; bias-adjusted or hierarchical models 14

Two defaults deserve emphasis. First, report the prediction interval alongside the confidence interval for the mean whenever a random-effects model is used: the confidence interval describes uncertainty about the average effect, while the prediction interval describes where the true effect of a new comparable study is likely to fall, and the two can lead to very different practical conclusions. Second, prefer methods with honest small-sample behavior: the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment for the mean, and small-sample corrected robust variance estimation for dependent effects.

16.3 Common pitfalls

The following errors recur in published meta-analyses and in manuscripts sent for review.

  • Double counting. Using a shared control group several times, or entering several outcomes from the same participants as independent effects, understates standard errors (Chapter 6).
  • Mixing metrics without conversion. Pooling standardized mean differences computed from change scores with those from post-test scores, or correlations with partial correlations from regressions with different control sets, combines quantities that estimate different things.
  • Reading I² as the amount of heterogeneity. I² is a ratio that depends on study precision. Report tau and the prediction interval to describe how much effects vary.
  • Fishing for moderators. Testing many moderators on few studies produces false positives. Pre-specify, adjust for multiplicity, and treat post hoc findings as hypotheses.
  • Ecological inference. A study-level association between mean age and effect size says nothing reliable about whether older individuals benefit more.
  • Treating trim-and-fill as a correction. It is a sensitivity analysis resting on a strong assumption about why studies are missing. No single publication-bias method should be read as the answer.
  • Fixed-effect inference with heterogeneous studies. A common-effect model applied to heterogeneous studies produces confidence intervals that are too narrow for any claim beyond the included studies.
  • Ignoring risk of bias. Averaging high- and low-quality studies without examining whether results differ by risk of bias can let the weakest evidence dominate.

16.4 A compact analysis template

The code below runs the core steps of a univariate random-effects meta-analysis from raw data to diagnostics, using the BCG vaccine trials. Each step corresponds to a chapter, and each would be expanded in a real project.

library(metafor)

# 1. Effect sizes: log risk ratios and sampling variances (Chapter 3)
dat <- escalc(measure = "RR", ai = tpos, bi = tneg, ci = cpos, di = cneg,
              data = dat.bcg, slab = paste(author, year, sep = ", "))

# 2. Random-effects model with REML and Hartung-Knapp inference (Chapter 4)
res <- rma(yi, vi, data = dat, method = "REML", test = "knha")

The pooled risk ratio printed in step 3 is about 0.49, a substantial average protective effect of BCG, but the 95% prediction interval runs from about 0.14 to 1.76: in a new setting comparable to these trials, the vaccine could be highly protective or have no benefit at all. Step 5 shows that absolute latitude accounts for about 76% of the between-trial variance (the pseudo-R² printed by the model): trials farther from the equator report stronger protection. This is the typical shape of a careful answer. The average effect matters less than the question of where and for whom the effect holds, and the diagnostics in steps 6 and 7 show how much the conclusion depends on individual studies and on assumptions about missing ones.

16.5 Reporting checklist

Whatever the method, a report should let a reader reproduce the analysis and judge the certainty of its conclusions (Chapter 15). At a minimum it states the registered protocol and any deviations; presents the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram; tabulates study characteristics and risk-of-bias ratings; names the effect-size metric, model, heterogeneity estimator, and confidence-interval method; reports the pooled estimate with its confidence interval, tau, and the prediction interval; describes moderator, sensitivity, and publication-bias analyses, including those that did not change the conclusion; rates certainty with GRADE; and shares the extracted data and analysis code.

16.6 Further reading

The Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Higgins et al. 2019) remains the most complete practical reference for reviews of interventions, and Borenstein et al. (2021) is a clear conceptual introduction to the statistics of meta-analysis. The documentation and worked examples on the metafor website, and the CRAN Task View on Meta-Analysis, track current software. Methodological developments appear in Research Synthesis Methods, Psychological Methods, Statistics in Medicine, and the Journal of Economic Surveys.
